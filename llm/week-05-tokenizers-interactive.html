Tokenizers: from text to a model’s vocabulary

A deep, incremental walkthrough of the official lecture sequence: tokenization challenges, Byte Pair Encoding, Word-Piece, and Sentence-Piece with a unigram language model.

The one question that organizes this week How do we learn a moderate-sized vocabulary that handles unknown words efficiently and remains as language agnostic as possible?

Concept 1

Why building a vocabulary is difficult

Language modelling assigns probabilities to a sequence of tokens. Before the model can learn those probabilities, the text must be split consistently and every token must map to an ID.

Raw textHow are you?
→
Tokenizer{How, are, you}
→
Token IDs{1, 2, 3}
→
Embeddingsvectors
→
Language modelpredictions

The vocabulary V is the codebook that connects strings to IDs. Special tokens such as <go>, <stop>, <mask>, <sep>, and <cls> may be included according to the architecture and downstream task.

Vocabulary size

A larger vocabulary requires a larger embedding matrix and more computation for softmax probabilities. Reducing 250K word types to 50K controls that cost, but creates the next problem: OOV words.

Out-of-vocabulary (OOV)

A finite word vocabulary cannot contain every future name, number, technical term, or novel word. A useful tokenizer must still represent a word it never saw during vocabulary construction.

Misspellings and web text

Web-scraped corpora contain typos. Treating every misspelling as a separate word token wastes vocabulary entries and learns brittle representations.

Open vocabulary

Names, numbers and productive word formation—especially in agglutinative languages—make the possible word vocabulary effectively unbounded.

Important trade-off Larger vocabulary → fewer tokens per sentence, but a larger embedding/output space. Smaller vocabulary → more reusable units, but longer sequences. The slides’ “wishlist” is a moderate vocabulary, efficient unknown-word handling, and language agnosticism.

Concept 2

Word, character, or subword?

The unit we choose controls vocabulary size, sequence length, and what happens to unseen words.

Word level

Whole words

Strength: short sequences and interpretable units.

Failure: huge vocabulary; enjoy and enjoyed become unrelated entries; unseen words become OOV.

Character level

Individual characters

Strength: small stable vocabulary and no ordinary unknown-word problem.

Failure: very long sequences. The model must reconstruct useful chunks from many positions.

Subword level

Learned reusable pieces

Strength: frequent words/chunks remain intact; rare words split into known pieces. This is the course’s preferred middle ground for LLMs.

Concept 3

Normalization, pre-tokenization, and post-processing

Subword tokenization is one stage in a wider text-processing pipeline.

Normalization makes superficially different text more consistent—for example, case treatment, accent removal, repeated-whitespace cleanup, and HTML handling. Pre-tokenization is the preliminary split before the subword algorithm. The slides explicitly note that whitespace splitting was traditionally called tokenization, but becomes pre-tokenization when followed by a subword algorithm.

Post-processing can add architecture-required symbols. This separates general vocabulary learning from the exact input format expected by the model.

Do not mix stages in an exam answerAdding [CLS]/[SEP] is special-token processing; splitting into subword units is tokenizer work; positional information belongs to the model input representation. The August 2025 PYQ deliberately places all three in a preprocessing-oriented question.

Concept 4

Byte Pair Encoding (BPE)

BPE learns a vocabulary bottom-up: begin with characters, repeatedly merge the most frequent adjacent pair, and preserve the merge order for inference.

4.1 Represent the training corpus

Build a dictionary of distinct words and their corpus frequencies. Append </w> to identify a word boundary, then separate every word into its current symbols.

pair_count(α, β) = Σ word_frequency × occurrences_of_pair_in_word

If a word has frequency 5 and contains (t, h) twice, that word contributes 5 × 2 = 10. This is an actual BPE implementation-assignment check and a frequent source of wrong manual tables.

4.2 Learn ordered merge rules

  1. Count all adjacent pairs in the current word representations.
  2. Select the pair with maximum weighted frequency.
  3. Replace that pair everywhere with the concatenated symbol.
  4. Add the new symbol to the vocabulary and record its merge rank.
  5. Recompute pairs and repeat until the merge or vocabulary limit.
Why rank mattersA later merge may require a token created earlier. At inference, use the learned ranks—not an unordered set of legal merges. In the bonus assignment’s newer example, (e,w) at rank 3 is applied before (n,e) at rank 5.

Concept 5

Word-Piece: frequency relative to its parts

BPE asks which pair occurs most. Word-Piece also asks whether that pair is unusually strong compared with how often its two tokens occur individually.

score(α, β) = count(α, β)count(α) × count(β)

The denominator penalizes tokens that occur widely in other contexts. A moderately frequent pair made from rare components can outrank a high-count pair whose components appear everywhere.

BPE objective

arg max count(α,β)

“Which adjacent pair is most popular?”

Word-Piece objective

arg max score(α,β)

“Which pair is strong relative to its marginals?”

Course notation changes with contextThe lecture introduces continuation subwords using ## (a single # is drawn for illustration). The supplied GA/PYQ calculations instead explicitly ask you to append </w>. In an exam, follow the convention in the question.

Concept 6

Sentence-Piece and the unigram language model

Instead of committing only to a greedy merge history, score possible complete segmentations and choose the most probable one.

6.1 From one merge path to many candidate segmentations

With a vocabulary containing pieces such as h, e, l, o, he, el, ll, lo, and hell, hello can be segmented in several ways:

he | ll | oh | el | lohe | l | lohell | o

BPE’s ordered merges make its output greedy and deterministic for a learned rule set. Sentence-Piece’s unigram formulation treats the subword sequence as hidden and chooses the candidate with maximum likelihood.

P(x) = ∏i=1…n P(xi)    and    x* = arg maxx∈S(X) P(x)

Here, S(X) is the set of possible segmentations of input sequence X. Unlike whitespace-dependent BPE pre-tokenization, X may be a sequence of characters or words without spaces, so this formulation can be applied to languages without word delimiters.

6.2 How the vocabulary is learned

  1. Set the desired vocabulary size.
  2. Construct a reasonably large seed vocabulary using BPE or an Extended Suffix Array.
  3. E-step: estimate a probability for each token from frequency counts.
  4. M-step: use Viterbi to segment the corpus into optimal, maximum-log-likelihood segments.
  5. Recompute likelihoods and remove a fraction of low-likelihood pieces.
  6. Repeat estimation, segmentation, and shrinking until the target vocabulary size.
Roles to rememberEM learns with hidden segmentations. Viterbi efficiently finds the best segmentation instead of enumerating every candidate.

6.3 Viterbi and the official whereby example

Viterbi works left to right. At every character position, it compares legal pieces ending there, retains the best accumulated log-probability, and records a backpointer. Log probabilities turn products into sums.

6.4 Morfessor: the extension named in the lecture

The unigram model uses maximum likelihood estimation (MLE). Morfessor uses a maximum a posteriori (MAP) objective, allowing linguistically motivated prior knowledge and a small amount of annotated data. The slide’s sesquipedalophobia example illustrates why a linguistically preferred segmentation may differ from the one selected by likelihood alone.

One-page revision

Week 5 cheat sheet

The motivation

Word tokens: short sequences but huge/OOV-prone vocabulary. Character tokens: small/open vocabulary but long sequences. Subwords preserve frequent chunks and decompose rare words.

Processing order

Raw text → normalization → pre-tokenization → subword tokenization → post-processing/special tokens → IDs → model.

BPE

Append </w> when instructed. Count adjacent pairs with word-frequency and within-word multiplicity. Merge the maximum-count pair. Add one vocabulary token per merge. Recompute. Store ordered merge ranks and replay them at inference.

Word-Piece

score(α,β) = count(α,β) / [count(α)count(β)]. Merge the maximum score—not necessarily the maximum count. If a marginal is unavailable, the answer may be “insufficient information.”

Sentence-Piece unigram

P(x)=∏P(xᵢ); choose x* with maximum probability/log-likelihood. Start with a large seed vocabulary, estimate probabilities with EM, find best segmentations with Viterbi, prune low-likelihood pieces, repeat. Backpointers recover the final segmentation.
