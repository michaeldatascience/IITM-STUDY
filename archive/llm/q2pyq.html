B1Initial vocabulary size Mar25 Q14 · Dec24 Q3 · Aug25 Q14 · W5 Q4
How many tokens are there in the initial vocabulary?
Direction Collect every distinct character across all four words — repeats of a word contribute nothing extra. Then add one for </w>. Word frequencies are irrelevant here.
Answer & theory

Theory

V₀ = the starting vocabulary, before any merge. It is the set of single characters plus the end-of-word symbol. At this point every word is spelled out letter by letter.

|Vk| = |V0| + k   (one new token per merge, nothing ever deleted)

Working

taught   → t a u g h
laughter → l a u g h t e r     new: l, e, r
drought  → d r o u g h t       new: d, o
tough    → t o u g h           new: none

distinct chars: t a u g h l e r d o  = 10
+ </w>                                = 11

Other corpora, same question: deeper/keener/sweeter → 10 · gentler/brighter/crisper → 13 · low/older/finest/lowest/loneliest → 12.

B2Highest / lowest frequency pair the only card needing a full tally
Which of the following pairs has the highest frequency before any merge?
('u','g') · ('a','u') · ('g','h') · ('h','t') · ('w','e') · None
Direction
  1. Write each word as characters + </w>. A word of n symbols gives n−1 adjacent pairs — including the pair ending in </w>.
  2. Every pair inside a word contributes the word's frequency, not 1.
  3. Add the same pair across different words.
  4. Since this is an MCQ, you only need to score the five options — not all 25 pairs. That is the time-saver.
Answer & theory

The rule

freq(a,b) = Σwords (count of word) × (times (a,b) is adjacent in it)

Word frequency is never compared to anything — it only ever gets multiplied in when tallying a pair. Words don't compete; pairs do.

Working — score only the options

('u','g'): taught 2 + laughter 1 + drought 4 + tough 5 = 12
('g','h'): taught 2 + laughter 1 + drought 4 + tough 5 = 12   ← tie!
('a','u'): taught 2 + laughter 1                        = 3
('h','t'): taught 2 + laughter 1 + drought 4            = 7
('w','e'): no 'w' anywhere                              = 0

Tie-break: scan words in the order given, left to right. In t-a-u-g-h-t-</w> the pairs appear as (t,a), (a,u), (u,g), (g,h)… so ('u','g') wins.

Ties are the norm, not the exception. Dec24: three pairs at 18. Aug25: two at 12. W5 GA: three at 21. Never grab the first max you spot — always check whether others equal it, then apply first-occurrence.

Checksum to catch a dropped pair

Total pair slots must equal Σ (word count × (length+1−1)). For this corpus: 2·6 + 1·8 + 4·7 + 5·5 = 73. If your tally doesn't sum to that, you missed one — usually the </w> pair.

Lowest-frequency variants

deeper/keener/sweeter → ('p','e') = 5 · gentler/brighter/crisper → ('p','e') = 3 · low/older/finest/lowest/loneliest → ('w','</w>') = 4.

B4Which pair is merged at merge k Mar25 Q18
Which pair will be merged in the third merge? Enter as e.g. "abc".
Direction You must actually run the loop three times. After each merge, rebuild the pair table from the updated corpus — the merged symbol is now a single atom and can itself be half of the next pair. That is the whole difficulty of this question type.
Answer & theory

Theory — merges compound

After merging u+g into ug, the symbol ug is a legal left half of a new pair. Students who only ever pair characters get merge 2 wrong and everything after it collapses.

Working

merge 1: ('u','g') freq 12    [tie with ('g','h'); ug first]
         t a ug h t </w>  ·  l a ug h t e r </w>  ·  d r o ug h t </w>  ·  t o ug h </w>

merge 2: ('ug','h') freq 12   ← ug is now one symbol
         t a ugh t </w>   ·  l a ugh t e r </w>   ·  d r o ugh t </w>   ·  t o ugh </w>

merge 3: ('o','ugh') freq 9   [drought 4 + tough 5]
         → new token "ough"

Answer: ough. Merge 4 would be ('t','</w>') at freq 6.

B5Minimum merges until a 3-letter token Mar25 Q17
What is the minimum number of merges after which there is a token with exactly 3 English letters?
Direction Run the merges and watch the letter count of each new token. Careful: </w> is not an English letter, so a token like t</w> has one letter, not two.
Answer & theory
merge 1 → "ug"    2 letters
merge 2 → "ugh"   3 letters ✓

Answer: 2.

Why it's quick: each merge grows the longest token by at most the length of the other half. Starting from 1-letter tokens, reaching 3 letters needs at least 2 merges, and here the minimum is achieved. If the merges had been disjoint (say ab then cd), you'd need a third merge to combine them.

B6Tokens with frequency revised — two variants Mar25 Q19 · Dec24 Q7 · W5 Q8
(a) How many tokens in the vocabulary will have their frequency revised/reduced after the first merge?

(b) Assume the process is stopped after 4 merges. How many tokens from the initial vocabulary will have their frequency revised/reduced to 0?
Direction These look identical but are counted differently. For (a) think about how many tokens make up one pair. For (b), list your four merges, then collect the distinct symbols appearing inside those merge pairs that were in the original character vocabulary — merged tokens like ug don't count, they were never in V₀.
Answer & theory

(a) Always 2

A merge consumes exactly one pair = two tokens. Both members have their counts revised. The answer is 2 every single time this is asked, on every corpus.

(Dec 2024 asks it after merging ee, where literally only e changes — the key is still 2. Answer 2 and move on.)

(b) Count distinct V₀ symbols appearing in the merge list

merges:  (u,g)  (ug,h)  (o,ugh)  (t,</w>)

symbols appearing:  u, g, ug, h, o, ugh, t, </w>
of these, in V₀:    u, g, h, o, t, </w>         → 6
Do not verify this by counting characters that truly vanish. If you actually inspect the merged corpus, only u, g, h, o reach a true count of zero — t and </w> still survive in laughter and tough. The official key is 6, so the exam is applying the simple "symbols named in the merge list" rule. Use the exam's rule.

B8Tokenize a new word from a merge list Mar25 Q20 · Dec24 Q9 · Aug25 Q17 · W5 Q10
Assuming the algorithm was stopped after 4 merges, how will the word trought</w> be tokenized?
A. t, r, ough, t</w>   B. tr, o, ugh, t</w>   C. t, r, ou, gh, t</w>   D. t, r, ough, t, </w>   E. tr, ough, t, </w>
Direction
  1. Split the word into raw characters — always, no matter how much of it you recognise.
  2. Walk down the merge list in the order it was learned, one rule at a time. For each, ask only: does that exact pair sit adjacent right now? If yes merge, if no skip.
  3. Never longest-match, never greedy-by-length. The merge order is the only order.
Get your merge list from B4 first.
Answer & theory

Working

merges in order:  (u,g) → ug,  (ug,h) → ugh,  (o,ugh) → ough,  (t,</w>) → t</w>

start        t  r  o  u  g  h  t  </w>
1. ug    →   t  r  o [ug] h  t  </w>
2. ugh   →   t  r  o [ugh]  t  </w>
3. ough  →   t  r [ough]    t  </w>
4. t</w> →   t  r [ough]  [t</w>]

Option A:  t, r, ough, t</w>   (4 tokens)

Note merges 2 and 3 were only possible because the earlier merge fired first. ough did not exist as a pair until ugh was glued together.

The −1 variant (Dec 2024 Q10)

Corpus {simulation, suspicious, stringent}, merges io, us, si, sim. Tokenize stereotypical</w> → it contains y, which never appears in the training corpus, so it isn't in the base alphabet → −1.

Always scan the base alphabet first. One foreign character means −1 regardless of what the merges would have done. This is also exactly why byte-level BPE exists — a 256-byte base alphabet makes −1 impossible.

Other worked instances: simultaneous</w> → 10 tokens · writer</w> with er, er</w>, ri → 4 tokens · finest</w> with es, est, est</w>, lo, low, lon, lone → [f, i, n, est</w>] = 4.

WordPiece & Unigram 2 cards

W1WordPiece highest-scoring pair Aug25 Q18 · W5 Q11–Q12
Corpus {gentler:4, brighter:5, crisper:3}, </w> appended. Which pair is merged first under score = count(α,β) / (count(α)·count(β))?
A. ('e','r')   B. ('r','</w>')   C. ('b','r')   D. ('r','i')
Direction
  1. You need two tallies now: adjacent-pair counts (as in BPE) and how often each single character appears anywhere, both weighted by word frequency.
  2. Score only the four options — do not build the whole table.
  3. If options tie, apply the same first-occurrence tie-break as BPE.
Answer & theory

Why the denominator exists

BPE merges pairs that are frequent because their halves are individually frequent. WordPiece asks the sharper question: does this pair co-occur more than chance predicts? Dividing by count(α)·count(β) penalises pairs built from very common characters.

Character counts (weighted)

r: 20   e: 16   </w>: 12   g: 9   t: 9   i: 8   b: 5   h: 5   n: 4   l: 4   c,s,p: 3

Score the options only

paircount(αβ)denominatorscore
('e','r')1216 × 20 = 3200.0375
('r','</w>')1220 × 12 = 2400.0500
('b','r')55 × 20 = 1000.0500
('r','i')820 × 8 = 1600.0500

Three-way tie at 0.05. First occurrence: ('r','</w>') appears in gentler (word 1); ('b','r') and ('r','i') only in brighter → Option B.

Notice ('e','r') loses despite tying on raw count (12). BPE would have merged it — it's the joint winner there. WordPiece rejects it because e is common (16). That divergence is the entire point of the question.

Week 5 GA variant — compute the score value

Corpus {low:4, older:5, finest:6, lowest:7, loneliest:8}. Highest scorer is fi:

count(fi) = 6 ,  count(f) = 6 ,  count(i) = 6 + 8 = 14
score = 6 / (6 × 14) = 1/14 = 0.0714  →  100·s = 7.14

(s,t) has count 21 — over three times higher — and still loses at 0.0476, because s and t are everywhere.

U1Unigram / Viterbi best segmentation Aug25 Q7
Input peanutbutter. Log-probs: pea −1.0, nut −0.8, but −1.2, ter −0.7, butter −1.0, peanut −0.5, t −2.0, er −1.5. Which tokenization is most probable?
A. [peanut,but,ter]   B. [pea,nut,butter]   C. [peanut,butter]   D. [pea,nut,but,ter]
Direction Log-probabilities add. Since all four candidates are handed to you, just sum each one and pick the least negative. Build the full dynamic-programming table only if no options are given.
Answer & theory

Theory

log P(s₁…sk) = Σ log p(si)

Pieces are assumed independent, so the score decomposes into a plain sum — which is exactly what makes dynamic programming (Viterbi) valid.

best[i] = maxj<i ( best[j] + log p(x[j:i]) )

Working — just sum the options

[peanut, butter]      −0.5 −1.0            = −1.5  ← highest
[peanut, but, ter]    −0.5 −1.2 −0.7       = −2.4
[pea, nut, butter]    −1.0 −0.8 −1.0       = −2.8
[pea, nut, but, ter]  −1.0 −0.8 −1.2 −0.7  = −3.7

Answer: C.

Where Unigram sits

BPE and WordPiece are greedy and constructive — build up merges, never undo. Unigram starts with a large candidate set and prunes the pieces whose removal costs least likelihood. SentencePiece is not a fourth algorithm — it's a framework wrapping BPE or Unigram that reads raw text as a stream (no whitespace splitting needed, so it works for Japanese) and encodes spaces as ▁ so detokenization is lossless.

Tokenization theory — rapid fire 1 card, 7 questions

T1The recurring MCQs all 3 papers + W5 GA
1. Primary motivation for subword tokenization over word-level?
2. Assertion: each BPE merge adds exactly one new token. Reason: BPE reduces the size of the initial vocabulary by merging frequent tokens.
3. Assertion: a small vocabulary is desirable, it reduces the embedding matrix. Reason: decreasing vocabulary size increases the number of tokens in the input.
4. Which represent normalization steps in building a tokenizer?
5. Key challenges in building a vocabulary?
6. What is the order of the language modeling pipeline?
7. For which language is BPE with whitespace pre-tokenization NOT feasible?
Direction For 2 and 3, evaluate the Assertion and the Reason separately before asking whether one explains the other — most wrong answers come from judging them as a unit. For 4, "normalization" means cleaning the raw text, before any splitting or ID assignment.
Answers & theory

1 — Subword motivation

It helps handle out-of-vocabulary words more effectively. Word-level has a hard OOV problem and a huge |V|; character-level has no OOV but 5× longer sequences (and attention is O(T²)). Subword is the negotiated middle.

2 — Assertion true, Reason false

Each merge appends one concatenated symbol and deletes nothing, so |V| strictly grows. BPE shrinks the corpus token count, never the vocabulary. → C

3 — Assertion false, Reason true

Reason is true: a smaller vocabulary means words break into more pieces, so input sequences get longer. Assertion is false as a blanket claim — a small vocabulary is not simply "desirable", because the longer sequences it causes cost you O(T²) attention. It's a trade-off, not a free win. → D

4 — Normalization = cleaning the raw string

Removing accents and lowercasing.
Not: assigning integers to tokens (that's the vocabulary/ID step), splitting on whitespace (that's pre-tokenization), adding special tokens (that's post-processing).

Pipeline inside a tokenizer: normalization → pre-tokenization → model (BPE/WordPiece/Unigram) → post-processing.

5 — Vocabulary challenges

A larger vocabulary means a bigger embedding matrix and heavier softmax ✓ · difficulty handling OOV words ✓
False: "embedding vector size increases with number of OOV words" (vector width d is a fixed hyper-parameter, unrelated to OOV) · "more entries always guarantee better generalization".

W5 GA phrases it as a list — there the answer is deciding vocabulary size, handling unknown tokens, misspelled words, names and numbers (all four).

6 — Pipeline order

Tokenizer → IDs → model → prediction → tokenizer again to convert back to text. The tokenizer bookends the model; it is used on the way in and on the way out.

7 — Japanese

Japanese has no whitespace word delimiters, so the pre-tokenization step BPE assumes doesn't exist. This is precisely the problem SentencePiece solves.

Bonus — character-level statements (W5 GA Q2)

True: softmax is easy (|V| ≈ 100) · vocabulary doesn't expand with new text · no unknown-token problem. False: "|V| larger than word-level". The real cost of character-level is sequence length, not vocabulary.

Sentinel tokens & pretraining objectives 3 cards

S1Sentinel sequence — target vs input Dec24 Q13 (target) · Mar25 Q7 (input) · W7 Q3
Strikeout words are dropped from the original sentence. Dec 2024 asks for the correct target sequence with sentinel tokens; Mar 2025 asks for the correct input sequence. Options differ only in (i) sentinel naming — [a][b][c] vs [v][w][x], (ii) whether the sequence ends with [z], (iii) span order.
Direction Read which one is asked — target or input. Then apply one binary rule about the terminal [z]. And check the option list for a naming twin: these are multiple-select, and both naming schemes are usually correct.
Answers & theory

The rule

Original: The quick brown fox jumps over the lazy dog
Drop:     "brown"  and  "jumps"

INPUT :  The quick <X> fox <Y> over the lazy dog     ← no terminal sentinel
TARGET:  <X> brown <Y> jumps <Z>                     ← ends with terminal sentinel
  • Input — dropped spans replaced in place by distinct sentinels. No [z] at the end.
  • Target — each sentinel followed by the span it replaced, in original order, then a final [z] marking end of output.
  • Naming is free — only order and the terminal sentinel are graded.
target ⇒ ends with [z] · input ⇒ does not

Dec 2024 (target) — correct: B and F

Both [v]…[y] … [z] and [a]…[d] … [z]. Options ending in [e] or with no terminal, or with spans reordered, are wrong.

Mar 2025 (input) — correct: B and C

The two options lacking the trailing sentinel. Options ending [e] or [z] are the target format, and the reordered option is wrong.

This is where marks leak. These are MSQs with two correct options that differ only in sentinel letters. Picking only the "natural-looking" one scores 1/2. Whenever an MSQ offers cosmetic variants, check whether the variation is actually graded.

Related — sentinels in the vocabulary (W7 GA Q4)

True. They are real embedded tokens (<extra_id_0>…) added at vocabulary construction. They must be, or the decoder could never emit them.

S2Objectives, formats, and which model does what Aug25 Q6 & Q11 · Mar25 Q9
1. What format of output is T5 expected to generate during span-corruption pretraining?
2. Select the correct input-output representations for pretraining objectives, base sentence "The quick brown fox jumps over the lazy dog" (span corruption / CLM / MLM / prefix-LM).
3. Which models use a form of denoising objective? (BERT · GPT-3 · BART · T5)
Direction For 2, check each option against the definition of its own objective — the wrong options are ones where the input/output pair doesn't match the objective it's labelled with. For 3, "denoising" means corrupt the input, then reconstruct — ask which models are trained that way, and remember MLM counts.
Answers & theory

1 — T5 output format

A concatenation of the masked spans in order, each preceded by its sentinel token. Not the full sentence with blanks filled in — that would be BART.

2 — Which representations are correct

ObjectiveVerdictWhy
Span corruption: in The <X> fox … <Y> dog, out <X> quick brown <Y> lazy <Z> ✓sentinels in place, spans in order, ends [Z]
CLM: in …the lazy, out The quick … lazy dog ✗CLM predicts the next token (dog), not a copy of the whole sentence
MLM: in The [MASK] fox …, out quick brown ✗one [MASK] stands for one token, not a two-word span
Prefix-LM: in finish the sentence: The quick brown fox jumps, out over the lazy dog ✓bidirectional prefix, then generate the continuation

3 — Denoising models

BERT, BART, T5. All three corrupt the input and reconstruct: BERT masks tokens (MLM is a denoising objective), BART corrupts and rebuilds the whole sequence, T5 corrupts spans and emits only the spans. GPT-3 is not — causal language modelling predicts the next token from clean text; nothing is corrupted.

M1Prefix-LM mask — counting non-infinity entries Dec24 Q14 · Mar25 Q13
Sequence length 32, first 2 tokens are the task-specific prefix. How many non-infinity elements are in the attention mask?
(March 2025 asks the same with length 64 and prefix 3.)
Direction Don't memorise a formula — build it. Two regions: the prefix tokens see all prefix tokens (bidirectional), and every token after the prefix sees everything up to and including itself (causal). Count each region, add. Sanity-check by setting prefix = 0 and prefix = T.
Answer & theory

Derivation

count = p·p  +  Σi=p+1T i  =  T(T+1)/2  +  p(p−1)/2

The second form reads as: the plain causal count, plus the extra upper-triangle entries opened up inside the prefix block.

Sanity checks: p = 0 → T(T+1)/2, pure causal ✓.   p = T → T², fully bidirectional, i.e. a BERT encoder ✓.

Dec 2024: T = 32, p = 2

32·33/2 + 2·1/2  =  528 + 1  =  529   ← matches the official key

Mar 2025: T = 64, p = 3

64·65/2 + 3·2/2  =  2080 + 3  =  2083
The March 2025 key says 2115, and it does not follow from any consistent reading of the mask. I checked several alternative visibility rules — prefix rows seeing the full sequence, suffix rows counted from the prefix boundary, and others — and every one of them also lands on 2083 for (64, 3) while still giving 529 for (32, 2). Since the Dec 2024 key independently confirms 529, the formula is sound and 2115 appears to be an error in that paper.

What to do in the hall: compute the formula. If the number you get isn't among the options (MCQ), pick the nearest; if it's numeric-entry, enter your formula answer. Know that 2115 exists in one key so you're not thrown by it.

Related mask facts

A causal mask sets scores to −∞ before softmax, so e−∞ = 0 — attention weight on a future token is exactly 0, no exponentials needed. A valid CLM attention matrix must be zero strictly above the diagonal and have rows summing to 1.

BART loss & decoding 2 cards

D1BART greedy-decoding loss Mar25 Q12 · W7 GA Q1 — 4 marks
Vocabulary V = ([start], deep, hot, is, learning, research, topic, very, [end]). Original sentence "[start] deep learning is very hot research topic [end]", corrupted "[start] deep [mask] is [mask] very hot [mask] topic [end]". An 8×9 matrix Ŷ of prediction probabilities is given, row 0 being the distribution after [start]. What is the loss value?
Direction
  1. Number the vocabulary columns in the order V is written. Column j ↔ token j.
  2. Write out the target sequence — the original uncorrupted sentence, one token per row.
  3. For each row, read off the probability sitting in the gold token's column.
  4. Take negative natural logs — and then be very careful about the last operation. Sum, or mean?
Answer & theory
L = −(1/n) Σt=1n ln p(goldt)

where: n = number of prediction steps (rows); ln = natural log; goldt = the token the model was supposed to produce at step t.

MEAN, not sum. This is the single most expensive error in the whole syllabus — it is off by a factor of n. In the Week 7 GA the sum was 7.62 and the correct answer was 1.09; a submission of 7.6 scored zero on a 4-mark question.

D2Top-k sampling & determinism 2026-style Q18 · Aug25-adjacent

Answers & theory

1 — Renormalization

P′(t) = P(t) / Σt′ ∈ top-k P(t′)

The discarded mass is redistributed proportionally among survivors, never just dropped.

F3Multi-task mixing with a cap Aug25 Q12
Proportional mixing: r_m = min(e_m, K) / Σ min(e_n, K). Task A has 10,000 examples, Task B has 1,000,000. What is the largest K such that neither task has sampling probability above 0.5?
Direction Try K equal to the smaller dataset size and see what both probabilities become. Then nudge K up by exactly 1 and watch which task breaks. That tells you whether you've found the boundary.
Answer & theory

Purpose of the cap

Without a cap, a task with 100× more data gets sampled 100× more often and dominates training. min(e, K) truncates every task at K, so oversized datasets stop growing their share.

Working

K = 10,000:  min(10000,10000) = 10000
             min(1e6,  10000) = 10000
             r = 10000/20000 = 0.5 each          ✓ exactly at the limit

K = 10,001:  min(10000,10001) = 10000
             min(1e6,  10001) = 10001
             r_B = 10001/20001 > 0.5             ✗ breaks

Answer: K = 10,000.

The general result worth carrying

The cap that makes the distribution uniform is exactly the size of the smallest dataset. Once K reaches it, every task is capped identically. Three tasks of 5k / 50k / 500k → K = 5,000 gives r = 1/3 each.

Temperature mixing (a related question): proportions raised to 1/T. As T → ∞, 1/T → 0, every proportion → 1, so the distribution becomes uniform across tasks.

Answers & theory

1 — The governing sentence

Both model size and dataset size must be increased appropriately, together. Growing one alone hits a wall set by the other. Always false: "training longer always helps", "larger models need less data", "model size alone suffices".

2 — 4× compute

Compute C ≈ 6ND, and optimal scaling grows N and D roughly equally, so 4× compute ⇒ ≈2× parameters and ≈2× data. Not 4× of either one alone.

tokens seen = steps × B × T

L2Loss ratio after doubling N and D Aug25 Q13 — 3 marks
L(N,D) = A^(−α_N) + B^(−α_D) where A = N/N_c, B = D/D_c, with N_c = 8.8×10¹³, D_c = 5.4×10¹³, α_N = 0.076, α_D = 0.095. Assume A = 2B. Find L(2N,2D) / L(N,D) to 3 decimals.
Direction Don't substitute the big constants. Ask what happens to each term separately when its argument doubles — a power of a doubled quantity factorises. Once you see the two constants that pop out, you'll notice the ratio is trapped between them, and you can almost read off the answer.
Answer & theory

The elegant route

L(2N,2D) = (2A)^(−α_N) + (2B)^(−α_D)
         = 2^(−α_N)·A^(−α_N)  +  2^(−α_D)·B^(−α_D)

So the ratio is a weighted average of two constants:

L(2N,2D)/L(N,D) = λ · 2−0.076 + (1−λ) · 2−0.095 = λ · 0.9487 + (1−λ) · 0.9363

Because it's a convex combination, the answer is pinned to [0.936, 0.949] no matter what A and B are. Evaluating with A = 2B gives 0.942 (I checked B from 0.5 to 100 — it moves only in the fourth decimal). Keyed range: 0.914–0.975.

You never need N_c or D_c here. If you're plugging in 8.8×10¹³ you've taken the long road and are likely to slip.

Generalisation

Scaling both by a factor f gives a ratio inside [f−α_D, f−α_N]. For f = 4 that's [0.877, 0.900] — exactly the square of the doubling interval.

LLM Quiz 2 — Weeks 5 · 7 · 8 — Page 1 of 2

BPE — the loop

  • Append </w>. |V₀| = distinct characters + 1
  • n symbols in a word → n−1 pairs, including the one ending in </w>
  • freq(a,b) = Σ (word count) × (times adjacent). Word freq is only ever multiplied in — words never compete, pairs do
  • |Vk| = |V₀| + k. Nothing is ever deleted
  • Corpus token count −1 per merge occurrence
  • Tie-break: pair whose first occurrence is earliest, scanning words in given order, L→R. Ties are the norm
  • Merged tokens are immediately merge candidates: (u,g)→ug, then (ug,h)→ugh
  • "Tokens with revised frequency after 1 merge" = 2 (size of the pair)
  • "V₀ tokens reduced to 0 after k merges" = distinct V₀ symbols named in the merge list

BPE — encoding a new word

  • Split into raw characters, always
  • Replay merges IN LEARNED ORDER. Not longest-match. Check every rule, skip non-matches
  • Any character missing from the base alphabet ⇒ −1
  • Byte-level BPE (256-byte base) ⇒ −1 impossible

score(a,b) = count(ab) / (count(a)·count(b))
  • count(a) = occurrences anywhere, weighted
  • Denominator penalises common characters ⇒ rare-character pairs win
  • MCQ: score only the options, then tie-break as in BPE

  • Encode: longest-match-first from left, ## continuations

Unigram / SentencePiece

best[i] = maxj<i ( best[j] + log p(x[j:i]) )
  • Log-probs add; pick the least negative. If options given, just sum all four
  • peanutbutter → [peanut, butter] = −1.5
  • SentencePiece = framework over BPE/Unigram; whitespace → ▁; lossless; no pre-tokenization (works for Japanese)
  • BPE = frequency · WordPiece = co-occurrence over chance · Unigram = likelihood + pruning

Tokenization theory

  • Subword motivation = handles OOV effectively
  • BPE assertion/reason → Assertion true, Reason false (|V| grows, never shrinks)
  • Small-vocabulary assertion/reason → Assertion false, Reason true

  • Tokenizer internals: normalization → pre-tokenization → model → post-processing
